    Introduction

    Well, would you look at that.

    The first two weeks of 2026 are already over, and all of my AP work made sure the time flew by. Nevertheless, I started working through TryHackMe's Jr. Penetration Tester pathway and knocked out the first two modules: Intro to Cybersecurity and Intro to Pentesting. As expected, they were pretty basic and foundational—most of it was recap from my Security+ coursework at Wake Tech—but there were still some solid takeaways, a couple of realizations, and one important decision I had to make about certifications.


    The very first room in the pathway was an offensive security intro where they had me hack my first machine. I initially didn't realize it was a guided room and tried to proceed completely on my own. I figured it would probably be a simple "inspect element" kind of problem and that I could knock it out real quick.

    As per usual, I was wrong.

    I poked around the target website and quickly realized it was just one page. Every link on it looped back to the same page, and there was nothing interesting in the page source as I'd initially hoped—no conveniently placed URLs I could visit.

    That's when it clicked: as if the stars had aligned, I needed to use gobuster to actually see what else was there—the same tool I almost failed to even install during middle school. This time, I used it correctly, not like in 8th grade when I was messing with it on my phone.

    The point that stuck with me this time was:
    Enumeration isn't optional. You can't hack what you can't see.

    It feels obvious now (and it kind of is), but at the time I felt lost because there was nothing immediately useful staring me in the face. The lesson here was clear: know the full scope of what's actually there before you start trying to exploit anything. You can't find the hidden admin panel if you don't know it exists.

    After running gobuster and discovering the hidden directory, the rest of the room was super straightforward. But that initial moment of "wait a minute, what am I even looking for" was important. It reinforced the methodology that I'd heard about, and even used a couple times, but never truly internalized: recon comes before action, always.


    All it took was: 
    gobuster dir -u http://(target IP) -w /usr/share/wordlists/dirb/common.txt

    Simple command, but using it at the right time made all the difference.


    One of the things I want to improve on this year, unrelated from security, is not overthinking everything. But alas, if you know me, you know that I just can't let go of something this important to me without doing way too much research first. Thus, the main struggle these two weeks wasn't technical—it was figuring out whether I was even on the right path with TryHackMe's PT1 certification.

    I noticed that the Jr. Penetration Tester pathway leads to the PT1 cert, so naturally I went down a research rabbit hole trying to figure out if it was actually worth pursuing or if I should pivot to something like eJPT or PJPT instead.

    Here's what I found:

    • eJPT and PJPT are significantly more established: they have bigger name recognition in the industry right now (especially eJPT).
    • PT1 is brand new: it's heavily reliant on TryHackMe rooms and doesn't have the same reputation yet.
    • Neither is perfect: eJPT has really solid prep materials but the exam itself is considered easier and more foundational. PT1's exam covers way more ground which may make it more impressive to earn, but the THM pathway alone probably won't be enough to pass it.
    • Cost matters: an INE subscription for eJPT prep is expensive. The PT1 pathway on TryHackMe is cheaper, but I realized that the majority of the rooms are premium, so I need to keep up my pace of one module per week or this'll get pricey fast.
    • Neither one guarantees a job: seeing as both are junior-level certifications, most online sources emphasize that PT1 and eJPT are stepping stones to higher-level certs like OSCP, which is more important in the long term anyway.

    After considering everything I could, I decided to stick with PT1. While it is newer and less acclaimed, it exposes me to a wider range of content. If a junior pentest cert is a stepping stone to an intermediate or advanced cert, then I think I'd prefer to stand on a wider foundation and plug a few holes, rather than standing on a shinier but smaller foundation where I could lose my balance. Plus, if I'm still documenting everything along the way, the cert itself is really just a bonus—the real value for me is in the skills I build while trying to figure all this stuff out.


    Most of what I covered in these first two modules was review—CIA triad, access control models, the pentesting methodology at a surface level. That said, there were a few new pieces of knowledge that filled in gaps I didn't even know I had.

    For example, what a SIEM actually is: I'd heard the term "SIEM" used a lot in my SEC-110 class, but we never really dug into what it feels like to use one. TryHackMe's defensive security intro gave me a high-level walkthrough of how security teams actually use SIEM tools to detect and respond to threats in real time. It's one thing to know the acronym; it's another to understand what it means in practice.

    In the Principles of Security module, I learned about Privileged Identity Management (PIM) and Privileged Access Management (PAM) for the first time. I'd worked with access control concepts before, both more basic and more complex, but I didn't know there were formal frameworks specifically for managing those concepts.

    I also learned that the Bell-LaPadula and Biba models—concepts I vaguely understood—actually have formal names and specific use cases. Bell-LaPadula is all about confidentiality (no read up, no write down), while Biba focuses on integrity (no write up, no read down). Having the proper terminology makes it way easier to talk about these things and understand when to apply them.

    Essentially, SEC-110 taught me that these concepts existed; TryHackMe taught me their actual names and when and where to use them.


    Next up is Module 3: Introduction to Web Hacking. This is where things start getting more interesting—actual web application vulnerabilities (that my own code is probably full of), OWASP Top 10, all that good stuff. I'm genuinely excited for this one because web app security is where a lot of the real-world action is, and as an amateur web developer, it's probably something I should have learned about sooner.

    After that comes a small crash course in Burp Suite (Module 4). The plan is to focus on module 3 this coming week and then wrap up web security with module 4 the following week. Since module 4 seems to be significantly shorter than module 3, I also plan to do at least one standalone box writeup that week—something beyond the guided rooms. I want to start applying what I'm learning independently, not just following walkthroughs.

    As for how I'm feeling about progress: honestly, pretty good. Sure, these first two modules were mostly review, but that's not a bad thing. It means I have a solid foundation to build on, and I'm already starting to see the gaps in my knowledge fill in. The real test will be when I get into unfamiliar territory and have to figure things out on my own.

    I have a feeling that unfamiliar territory is closer than I think—let's see what web hacking can throw at me.
